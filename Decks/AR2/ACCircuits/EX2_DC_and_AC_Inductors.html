Introduction to Inductors · Topic 01

What Is an Inductor?

In the DC Circuit Fundamentals course you saw that current in a wire produces a magnetic field, and that winding the wire into a coil around an iron core makes that field much stronger. This coil is the basis of solenoids and motors.

A coil of wire used in an ac circuit is called an inductor. Like a capacitor, almost none of the power supplied to it does immediate work. Its two main jobs are to produce a magnetic field and to oppose current changes in the circuit.

Inductor
general symbol
Magnetic-core inductor
the most common type; the bars mark the iron core
Resistors, capacitors, and inductors all oppose current flow. They just do it in different ways: a resistor dissipates energy, a capacitor stores it in an electric field, an inductor stores it in a magnetic field.

The Resistor Reference Circuit

This circuit has an ordinary resistor in parallel with the indicator light.

Closing the switch sends a current through the circuit that splits between the resistor and the light.
The split depends only on their resistances, and each current is constant.
So the light reaches its normal glow immediately and holds it steady.

Switch closes

The Light Flares, Then Fades to Dim

Voltage is applied to the inductor and its magnetic field starts to build.
A voltage is induced across the inductor with polarity opposing the source, which opposes current in the inductor.
At first little current takes the inductor path, so more flows through the light: it glows brighter than normal.
As the field reaches full strength the induced voltage falls, current shifts into the inductor, and the light fades to dimmer than normal.
Operation of Inductors · Topic 02 · DC · Turning Off
Switch opens

The Light Flashes Very Bright, Then Dies

Voltage is removed, so the inductor's magnetic field collapses.
The collapsing field induces a voltage across the inductor with the same polarity as the disconnected source.
That induced voltage drives a high current through the circuit, so the light briefly glows very bright.
As the field finishes collapsing the induced voltage drops to 0 V, current stops, and the light fades out.
An inductor always fights the change in current: it resists current rising when you switch on, and it keeps current flowing when you switch off.
Operation of Inductors · Topic 02 · AC

On AC, the Field Never Settles

  • During the positive half-wave the magnetic field builds up to a maximum, then collapses as the voltage returns to zero.
  • During the negative half-wave the field builds again with inverted polarity, then collapses.
  • This build-and-collapse repeats every cycle. Each time current tries to change, the induced voltage rises and the inductor opposes the change.
So an ac inductor opposes current, just like a resistor or capacitor. It does this by storing energy in its magnetic field as current rises and releasing it as current falls.
Inductance · Topic 03

Inductance L

The fundamental characteristic of an inductor is its inductance: a measure of its ability to store energy in its magnetic field. The higher the inductance, the more energy it can store.

Inductance is measured in henries (H), named after Joseph Henry, who discovered self-inductance.

Like resistors and capacitors, an inductor has a tolerance: the percentage by which its actual inductance may differ from its nominal rating.
Reactance of AC Inductors · Topic 04

Inductive Reactance XL

An ac inductor opposes current by storing and releasing magnetic energy. That opposition is called inductive reactance, measured in ohms (Ω), and it depends on the inductance and the source frequency.

A higher frequency or a larger inductance gives a larger reactance, so less current flows. This is the opposite trend to a capacitor.
Equation 11: inductive reactance
XL = 2π f L
f in hertz (Hz)
L in henries (H)
XL in ohms (Ω)
Equation 12: Ohm's law for an inductor
I = EXL
XL takes the place of resistance R.
Worked Example · Topic 04

220 V, 50 Hz, 3.5 H

Step 1: inductive reactance
XL = 2π fS L
= 2π × 50 × 3.5
XL = 1100 Ω
Step 2: source current
IS = ESXL
= 220 V1100 Ω
IS = 0.20 A
Every real inductor also has wire resistance. The wire is long, so this resistance is significant and adds to the reactance in opposing current.
Series and Parallel Inductors · Topic 05

Combining Inductors: Just Like Resistors

Equivalent inductance is found exactly as for resistors. You can then convert it to an equivalent reactance, or combine the individual reactances directly. This is the opposite of how capacitors combine.

Series
Leq = L1 + L2 + … + Ln  (Eq. 13)
Xeq = XL1 + XL2 + … + XLn  (Eq. 15)
Parallel
1Leq = 1L1 + 1L2 + … + 1Ln  (Eq. 14)
1Xeq = 1XL1 + 1XL2 + … + 1XLn  (Eq. 16)
Inductors in series add. Inductors in parallel give a smaller total. Their reactances follow the same pattern, and so does the resistance of series and parallel resistors.
Applications of Inductors · Topic 06

Where Inductors Show Up

  • Motors and generators. Their windings are essentially inductors. The interaction between the winding's magnetic field and the rest of the machine is the basic principle of how motors and generators work.
  • Transformers. Two inductors whose magnetic fields interact, used to change the voltage and current of an ac circuit. Transformers are covered in the next exercise.

The Parallel Fluorescent Lamp

  • The inductor is wired in parallel with a 120 V fluorescent lamp.
  • A fluorescent lamp needs a minimum striking voltage to light, and that voltage is far above the 24 V ac source.
  • During normal operation the lamp stays dark and its resistance is nearly infinite, so it can be ignored.
  • The lamp only flashes when the inductor's magnetic field collapses and induces a high voltage across it. That flash makes the inductor's behaviour visible.
