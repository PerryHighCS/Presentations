Introduction to AC Relays · Topic 01

From DC Relays to AC Relays

In the DC Circuit Fundamentals course, you saw that a relay is made of an electromagnet, usually a coil of wire wound around an iron core, and one or more sets of contacts. When current flows in the coil, the coil is energized and produces a magnetic field. That field pulls on the relay armature, which opens or closes the contacts. Each set of contacts acts like a switch in the circuit it is connected to.

An ac relay works the same way. The only difference is that the current in the coil is alternating current instead of direct current.

The armature is made of a ferromagnetic material, so it is attracted to the coil's magnetic field no matter which way that field points. Reversing the current every half-cycle therefore has no effect on how the relay operates.
Positive half-cycle: the armature is pulled in.
Negative half-cycle: still pulled in.
Introduction to AC Relays · Topic 01 · Chattering

The Problem at the Crossing

Twice every cycle, the ac current in the coil passes through zero. At those instants the coil is momentarily de-energized, so the pull on the armature drops to nothing.

The armature lets go for an instant, which makes a slight buzzing sound called chattering. It can also make the contacts very briefly open or close, which is highly undesirable.
AC relays solve this with a shading coil (or shading pole). It produces a second, delayed magnetic field, so some field is always present even when the main current passes through zero.

A Light Controlled by an AC Relay

In the control circuit, a toggle switch connects the ac source to the relay coil, CR. In the load circuit, the source feeds an indicator light through the relay's normally open (NO) contact, also labeled CR because it belongs to that coil.

Close the switch and watch the chain of events: the coil energizes, its field closes the NO contact, and the light turns on.

A coil in an ac circuit is an inductor, so the relay coil has an inductive reactance that limits the current flowing in it. Take that into account when you choose the ac source that powers the coil.

Contactors · Topic 02

A Relay Built for Big Currents

A common type of ac relay is the contactor. Its operating principles are virtually identical to those of any other relay: a coil is energized, and its magnetic field opens or closes a set of contacts.

The only feature that sets a contactor apart is its current rating, which is much higher than that of other types of relays.

Typical contactor current rating
from at least 10 A up to several hundred amperes
That high rating makes contactors ideal for controlling many types of high-power loads, such as the pump motor this contactor switches.
Applications of AC Relays · Topic 03

Where AC Relays Are Used

Just like dc relays, ac relays are used in a wide variety of applications as a replacement for switches. A relay is safer for the user to operate than a switch, because the control circuit for the relay coil is isolated from the circuit the relay controls. That isolation lets a much lower current flow in the control circuit than in the controlled circuit.

High-power loads

The isolation advantage matters most for contactors, which control high-power loads such as heavy-duty motors, lighting and heating equipment, and capacitor banks.

HVAC systems

Another common use of ac relays is in heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) systems, where a low-voltage thermostat circuit switches blowers, compressors, and heaters.

Pressing a small button on a 24 V control circuit can safely start a motor that draws tens of amperes in the load circuit. The person operating the button never touches the high-current circuit.
Applications of AC Relays · Topic 03 · Protection

Protection Relays

AC relays are also often used for circuit protection, in which case they are called protection relays. A protection relay is designed to open a circuit when certain conditions occur, which stops the current from flowing.

For example, a protection relay can prevent overvoltage.
As soon as the voltage applied to the relay coil reaches a set value, the coil becomes energized.
The relay contacts then open, disconnecting the circuit before the excess voltage can do damage.
The overload relay shown here protects a motor. It stops the motor as soon as certain predetermined ratings are exceeded.
Training System Component: the AC Relay · Topic 04

The AC Relay in the AC/DC Training System

Ratings
Coil voltage rating: 24 V
Contacts: two sets, each with an NO and an NC terminal
Each contact set: 125 V and 10 A maximum
Never apply more than 125 V across the contacts or let more than 10 A flow through them. Exceeding either rating could damage the ac relay and prevent its normal operation.

Reading the Front Panel

  • Applying 24 V across the coil energizes it.
  • Each set of contacts controls its own independent circuit, so one relay can switch two separate loads.
  • The NO and NC terminals of a set can be used at the same time. When the coil is energized, the circuit on the NC contact opens while the circuit on the NO contact closes.
NO contact
open until the coil is energized
NC contact
closed until the coil is energized
