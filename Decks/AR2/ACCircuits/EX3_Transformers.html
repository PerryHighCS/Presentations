Introduction to Transformers · Topic 01

What Is a Transformer?

A transformer is essentially two inductors, coils of wire, wound around a common core of ferromagnetic material such as iron. One coil is the primary winding and the other is the secondary winding. The two windings are electrically isolated from each other, so no current flows directly between them.

The primary winding is connected to the ac power source and the secondary winding is connected to the load. Because a transformer is a bidirectional device, either winding can be used as the primary or the secondary.

Transformers transfer power from one winding to the other through the magnetic field produced by each coil, and they can change the voltage and current between the windings. These properties make them common in ac circuits.
Transformer symbol
two coils separated by an iron core
Transformer Operation · Topic 02

How a Transformer Transfers Power

The ac source applies a voltage to the primary winding, so current flows in the primary circuit.
That current makes the primary winding produce a magnetic field in the iron core.
The changing magnetic field induces a voltage across the secondary winding.
The induced voltage is applied to the resistor, driving current in the secondary circuit.
Power crosses from primary to secondary by electromagnetic induction, with no electrical contact between the windings. This is why a transformer isolates the ac source from the load.
Turns, Voltage, and Current Ratios · Topic 03

The Three Ratios

The turns ratio compares the number of turns in the primary winding, NPri, with the number in the secondary winding, NSec, written NPri : NSec. It sets the ratio between the transformer's input and output values. The two windings in the detailed view have the same number of turns, so this transformer's turns ratio is 1 : 1.

Voltage ratio  ·  Eq. 17 & 18
EPriESec = NPriNSec
ESec = EPri × NSecNPri
Current ratio  ·  Eq. 19 & 20
IPriISec = NSecNPri
ISec = IPri × NPriNSec
The current ratio is inverted relative to the turns and voltage ratios. The winding with more turns carries the higher voltage but the lower current.

ISec = ESecR = 24 V60 Ω

With a 1 : 1 turns ratio the primary and secondary voltages are equal, and so are the currents.

Power In Equals Power Out

The power at each winding is the product of its voltage and its current. Using the values from the 1 : 1 circuit:

In theory the power at the secondary winding always equals the power at the primary winding, whatever the turns, voltage, and current ratios.
In practice the secondary power is always slightly lower. No transformer is 100% efficient, and some power is lost during the transfer from the primary winding to the secondary winding.

PPri = EPri × IPri = 24 V × 0.4 A
PPri = 9.6 W
Power at the secondary winding
PSec = ESec × ISec = 24 V × 0.4 A
PSec = 9.6 W
Step-Up and Step-Down Transformers · Topic 04

When the Turns Ratio Is Not 1 : 1

A 1 : 1 transformer leaves the voltage and current unchanged, but most transformers have a turns ratio other than 1 : 1. These are called step-up and step-down transformers, and they trade voltage for current between the primary and the secondary.

Step-up transformer
Fewer turns on the primary: NPri < NSec.
Raises the voltage, lowers the current.
Step-down transformer
More turns on the primary: NPri > NSec.
Lowers the voltage, raises the current.
Because power is conserved, raising the voltage by some factor must lower the current by the same factor, and lowering the voltage must raise the current. The power at the two windings stays equal.
Step-Up Transformer · Topic 04 · Turns Ratio 1 : 2

More Turns on the Secondary

Here the secondary winding has twice the turns of the primary, a turns ratio of 1 : 2. Connected to the same 24 V, 60 Ω circuit:

Voltage, current, power
ESec = 24 V × 21 = 48 V
ISec = 48 V60 Ω = 0.8 A  →  IPri = 0.8 A × 21 = 1.6 A
PPri = 24 V × 1.6 A = PSec = 48 V × 0.8 A = 38.4 W
Step-up characteristics: NPri < NSec, so EPri < ESec and IPri > ISec. The voltage rises, the current falls, and the power is unchanged.
Step-Down Transformer · Topic 04 · Turns Ratio 2 : 1

More Turns on the Primary

Here the primary winding has twice the turns of the secondary, a turns ratio of 2 : 1. Connected to the same 24 V, 60 Ω circuit:

Voltage, current, power
ESec = 24 V × 12 = 12 V
ISec = 12 V60 Ω = 0.2 A  →  IPri = 0.2 A × 12 = 0.1 A
PPri = 24 V × 0.1 A = PSec = 12 V × 0.2 A = 2.4 W
Step-down characteristics: NPri > NSec, so EPri > ESec and IPri < ISec. The voltage falls, the current rises, and the power is unchanged.
Transformer Voltage Regulation · Topic 05

Voltage Regulation

In a real transformer the secondary voltage drops as the secondary current rises, that is, as the load becomes heavier. Voltage regulation is the transformer's ability to hold the secondary voltage steady as the secondary current increases. The better the regulation, the smaller the drop.

Voltage regulation  ·  Eq. 21
Reg. (%) = ENL − EFLENL × 100%
ENL = no-load secondary voltage (secondary current is zero)
EFL = full-load secondary voltage (secondary current equals the current rating)
Types of Transformers · Topic 06

Three Common Types

Control transformer

Used where a steady voltage or current is needed. It has a low voltage and current rating, so it handles low power, and it is often used to step down the voltage in solenoid and relay circuits. It is generally well insulated.

Power transformer

Used to step up or step down the voltage of ac transmission lines. It has high voltage, current, and power ratings. Because a transformer is more efficient below its maximum ratings, a power transformer is often rated higher than necessary to keep losses low.

Isolation transformer

Built for maximum electrical insulation. It prevents electric shocks, suppresses electrical noise, and transfers power between circuits that must not be connected, such as around sensitive hospital equipment. It usually has a 1 : 1 turns ratio, so it barely changes the voltage and current.

Training System Component: the Transformer · Topic 07

The Transformer in the AC/DC Training System

Ratings
Type: isolation transformer, turns ratio 1 : 1
Voltage rating: 24 V    Power rating: 20 VA
Do not apply more than 24 V to the transformer or transfer more than 20 VA through it. Exceeding either rating could damage the transformer.

Reading the Ratings

  • The power rating is given in VA (volt-amperes), not watts. The value is simply the RMS current multiplied by the RMS voltage.
  • Because it is an isolation transformer with a 1 : 1 ratio, it has virtually no effect on the voltage and current in the circuit.
  • The winding insulation can be checked with an ohmmeter: the resistance between any primary terminal and any secondary terminal should read infinite.
