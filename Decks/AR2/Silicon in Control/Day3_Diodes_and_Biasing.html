The Mystery Component

It Is a Diode

The mystery part we saw last lesson is a diode: one PN junction in a small case. The band on the case marks the N side, which is called the cathode. The P side is the anode.

→ →

The symbol's arrow points the way conventional current flows when the diode conducts: from anode to cathode. Your LED from Day 1 is a diode too, which is why turning it around also turned it off.

Biasing the Junction

Two Ways to Connect a Battery

Forward bias: + to the anode

The battery pushes carriers toward the junction, and the depletion region shrinks. Above about 0.6 to 0.7 V, carriers flood across and current flows.

Reverse bias: + to the cathode

The battery pulls carriers away from the junction, and the depletion region widens. Almost no current flows, so the diode acts like an open switch.

Calculating · The 0.7 V Rule

Current in a Diode Circuit

A forward-biased silicon diode has a voltage drop of about 0.7 V, almost no matter how much current flows. The rest of the supply voltage drops across the resistor.

VR = 5 V − 0.7 V = 4.3 V
I = 4.3 V ÷ 1000 Ω = 4.3 mA
Turn the diode around and almost no current flows. The resistor drops 0 V, so the diode has the whole 5 V across it.

Wrap Up

What We Know, and What Comes Next

Forward bias

+ on the anode. The depletion region collapses above about 0.7 V, and the resistor sets the current.

Reverse bias

+ on the cathode. The depletion region widens, almost no current flows, and the whole supply appears across the diode.
