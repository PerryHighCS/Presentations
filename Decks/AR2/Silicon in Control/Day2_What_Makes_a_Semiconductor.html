Materials · How Freely Charge Moves

Conductors, Insulators, and Semiconductors

Conductor · copper

Each atom lets go of an electron, so the metal is full of free electrons that move at the smallest push.

Insulator · glass, rubber

Every electron is locked in a bond. Almost nothing can move, even with a large voltage.

Semiconductor · silicon

Electrons are locked in bonds, but a few break free, and we can add more on purpose.

Silicon · The Crystal

Silicon Shares Four Electrons

A silicon atom has four outer electrons. In a crystal, each atom shares one electron with each of its four neighbors, so every bond holds a pair of electrons.

With every electron locked in a bond, pure silicon at room temperature has very few free carriers, so it conducts poorly.

Heat can shake an electron loose. It leaves an empty spot in its bond called a hole. That is why silicon conducts better when it is warm.

The first transistors were made of germanium, which works the same way but leaks much more current when warm. That is one reason the industry moved to silicon.

Doping · N-Type Silicon

Phosphorus Adds a Free Electron

Doping means replacing a few silicon atoms with a different element on purpose.

A phosphorus atom has five outer electrons. Four fill the bonds, and the fifth has nowhere to go, so it wanders free.

The phosphorus atom is now a fixed positive ion (P⁺). It stays in place in the crystal while its electron moves.

Silicon with extra free electrons is N-type, for the negative charge carriers.

Doping · P-Type Silicon

Boron Leaves a Hole

A boron atom has only three outer electrons, so one of its four bonds is missing an electron. That empty spot is a hole.

A neighboring electron can jump into the hole. Now the empty spot is where that electron used to be, so the hole has moved the other way.

A hole acts like a positive charge that moves, so silicon with extra holes is P-type. The boron becomes a fixed negative ion (B⁻).

A hole is not a particle. It is a missing electron, and it moves only when real electrons move into it.

The PN Junction

Where P Meets N

  • Electrons near the boundary cross into the P side and fill holes. Both carriers disappear.
  • That leaves a band with no free carriers, only fixed ions: B⁻ on the P side and P⁺ on the N side. This band is the depletion region.
  • The fixed ions make an electric field that pushes carriers back, so the crossing stops. The junction now acts like a barrier.
