Concept · Amplifiers

What Is an Amplifier?

An amplifier takes a small, changing signal and makes a larger copy of it. The output rises and falls in step with the input, just bigger.

You use them every day: a guitar amp, a speaker system, or a tiny sensor signal boosted so a controller can read it.

A transistor can amplify a signal. A small change in IB makes a change in IC that is β times bigger, with the same shape.

But only in the right region. Push the transistor too far and the amplified copy gets its peaks clipped off.

Concept · Operating Regions

The Three Regions

How hard you drive the base decides the transistor's region. Each one has its own collector current and VCE, the voltage from collector to emitter.

Cutoff

No base current, so no collector current. An open switch.

Active

IC = β × IB. The output follows the input: an amplifier.

Saturation

The load limits IC, so it stops rising. A closed switch.

Your switch in the last lesson was saturated. That is why IC ÷ IB came out near 21 instead of β.

Concept · Power in the Transistor

Why a Switch Stays Out of the Active Region

The power a transistor turns into heat is P = IC × VCE. A switch wants one of the two to be nearly zero: no current in cutoff, almost no voltage in saturation.

LoadSaturatedHalfway on (active)
LED, 19 mA19 mA × 0.2 V = 4 mW10 mA × 1.5 V = 15 mW
Motor, 500 mA from 12 V0.5 A × 0.2 V = 0.1 W0.25 A × 6 V = 1.5 W

At 1.5 W a small transistor gets hot enough to fail. A switch should snap between cutoff and saturation.

An amplifier lives in the active region on purpose, because there the output current follows the input. It is designed to handle the heat.

Calculate · Biasing

Choosing a Base Resistor

Design the LED switch so it always saturates, even with the weakest 2N3904. The 2N3904 datasheet guarantees β (listed as hFE) of at least 100 near 10 mA.

1. Load currentIC(sat) = (5 VSupply voltage − 2 VLED voltage drop − 0.2 VVCE(sat): transistor fully on) ÷ 150 ΩLED current-limiting resistor = 18.7 mA
2. Minimum base currentIB(min) = 18.7 mAIC(sat) from step 1 ÷ 100Minimum β (hFE) from the datasheet = 0.19 mA
3. Drive 5 times harderIB ≈ 5Safety factor: drive 5 times harder × 0.19 mAIB(min) from step 2 ≈ 1 mA
4. Base resistorRB = (5 VSupply voltage − 0.7 VVBE: base-emitter diode drop) ÷ 1 mAIB from step 3 = 4.3 kΩ

The nearest standard value is 4.7 kΩ, which gives 0.91 mA, still almost 5 times the minimum. That is the resistor you used in the last lesson.

Never replace the base resistor with a wire. Without it, the base-emitter junction takes as much current as the supply can give, and it burns out.

Troubleshooting · The Dim LED

Find the Hidden Fault

The build sheet calls for a 2N3904, a 4.7 kΩ base resistor, and the emitter to ground. The input is on, but the LED is dim or dark. Something on the board does not match the build sheet, and you have 10 minutes of bench time.

1

Test. Each test costs minutes. The reading appears in your evidence log.

2

Rule out. Strike a cause only when a reading rules it out.

3

Diagnose. Choose the cause and the fix that cures it.

4

Defend. Tag the readings that prove it, then read your record.

Read the bench notes first: they hold leads. Some choices are unsafe; they cost time, are never carried out, and count against you.

Wrap Up

What We Know, and What Comes Next

A switch

Lives in cutoff or saturation. Size RB for the weakest β, then drive about 5 times harder.

An amplifier

Lives in the active region, where IC follows IB, and the transistor turns IC × VCE into heat.

Next class: a saturated transistor is a clean on/off switch. Put a few of them together, and you can build the logic gates that form the basis of digital circuits.
