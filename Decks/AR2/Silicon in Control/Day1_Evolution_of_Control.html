Limits of the Relay

The Cost of 10,000 Relays

Early computers really were built from relays. They worked, but four problems grew with every relay engineers added.

Speed

Moving the armature takes about 10 milliseconds, so a relay can switch only a few dozen times per second.

Wear

A typical relay is rated for about 10 million operations before its mechanism wears out.

Power

Each coil uses about 0.5 W while it is on. Ten thousand coils would use 5,000 W, as much as several space heaters.

Arcing

Contacts spark as they open. The sparks pit the metal, and over time the contacts stick or stop conducting.

In 1947, operators of the Harvard Mark II relay computer found a moth trapped in one of its relays. They taped it into their logbook as the first actual case of a bug being found.

Switching With No Moving Parts

Inside a Vacuum Tube

A vacuum tube controls current with electrons flying through empty space. Nothing inside it moves except the electrons.

A heater warms the cathode until electrons boil off its surface. The positive plate at the top attracts them, so current flows across the vacuum.

Between them sits the grid, a wire mesh. Making the grid slightly negative pushes electrons back toward the cathode, so a few volts on the grid control the much larger current in the plate circuit.

This tube, with three working parts, is called a triode. It was invented in 1906.

Vacuum Tubes at Scale

ENIAC: A Room Full of Tubes

ENIAC, finished in 1945, was one of the first general-purpose electronic computers. Its tubes switched thousands of times faster than any relay, but it had problems of its own.

Tubes

17,468 vacuum tubes, each with its own heater.

Power

About 150 kW, much of it turned straight into heat.

Failures

Heaters burn out like light bulbs. Even after improvements, a tube failed about every two days.

Each mark stands for about 50 tubes. Finding the one that failed meant searching a whole room.

Wrap Up

What We Know, and What Comes Next

Today

Relays switch by moving metal, so they are slow and wear out. Vacuum tubes switch with electrons in a vacuum, but their heaters waste power and burn out.

The mystery

The mystery component let current through in one direction only, with no moving parts, no vacuum, and no heater.

Next class: the mystery part is made from a crystal of silicon. What could be inside a solid crystal that lets current through one way but not the other?
