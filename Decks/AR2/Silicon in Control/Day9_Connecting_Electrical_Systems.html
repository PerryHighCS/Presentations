Hook · Two Different Worlds

A Logic Output Meets a Solenoid Valve

Your logic circuit has decided the valve should open. The logic runs on 5 V, and the valve is a 24 V industrial part like the ones on the Festo trainer.

A logic output5 V · about 20 mA

Enough to light an LED. Most logic outputs supply only a few milliamps, and a small current is all a logic input needs.

A 24 V solenoid valve24 V · 200 mA or more

Its coil needs about ten times the current at almost five times the voltage, and it is an inductor that kicks back when switched off.

Think: what is the worst thing that could happen if you wired the logic output straight to the valve and the 24 V supply?

Concept · The Interface

Three Problems an Interface Solves

The circuit between the logic and the load is called an interface. It has three problems to solve.

Not enough current

The logic output is too weak to run the coil. A transistor lets its small current control a large one, as on Day 5.

24 V in the wrong place

One wiring mistake or failed part can put 24 V on a 5 V chip and destroy it. Noise from motors and coils can also travel along shared wires.

The coil kicks back

Switching a coil off makes a spike of hundreds of volts. A flyback diode gives that current a safe loop, as on Day 4.

So an interface does two jobs: it amplifies the signal, and it isolates the logic from the 24 V side so that nothing but the signal crosses over.

Concept · Isolation

The Optocoupler

An optocoupler holds an LED and a phototransistor in one small package, facing each other across a clear gap.

Current through the LED makes light. The LED is infrared, so you cannot see it.

The light turns on the phototransistor, and current flows on the other side: the light plays the role of base current.

Only light crosses the gap. No wire connects the two sides, and the gap withstands thousands of volts.

Concept · Isolation

Each Side Has Its Own Ground

On Day 7 you had to join the LogiKIT ground to your breadboard ground, because the switch's current needed a path back. An optocoupler is the exception: the signal crosses as light, so the two grounds stay separate.

Why keep them apart

A short or a spike on the 24 V side has no path into the logic. Current from motors and coils stays on its own side.

How to measure

Put the meter's black lead on the ground of the side you are measuring. Between the two sides there is no shared reference, so the reading means nothing.

Joining the two grounds with a jumper "to be safe" throws the isolation away. The circuit still works, but a fault on the 24 V side can now reach the logic.

Concept · Choosing the Switch

Is a 2N3904 Enough?

Check the load against the transistor's ratings before you build. The 2N3904 is rated for 40 V and 200 mA, and its gain falls off well before 200 mA.

A relay coil

Under about 100 mA: the 2N3904 handles it. Today's coil draws about 50 mA.

A solenoid valve

Often 200 mA or more: use a logic-level MOSFET, which switches amps with almost no gate current.

The spike

A 24 V coil switched off without a diode spikes far above 40 V. Without D1, Q1 fails the first time it turns off.

Concept · Power Switching Devices

MOSFETs, SCRs, and TRIACs

Bigger loads use semiconductor switches built for power. They differ in what turns them on, what turns them off, and whether they work on DC or AC.

MOSFET

On: while the gate voltage is high. Off: when the gate goes low.

Used for: DC loads such as solenoid valves and motors.

SCR

On: one gate pulse, then it latches. Off: only when its current falls to zero.

Used for: controlling large DC or AC power, one direction only.

TRIAC

On: a gate pulse, in either direction. Off: at each zero crossing of the AC.

Used for: AC loads such as heaters, lamps, and fans.

Connect · Industrial Parts

The Solid-State Relay

Put an optocoupler and a TRIAC in one sealed block and you have a solid-state relay (SSR), a common part in control panels. A small DC signal on the input switches an AC load on the output, with full isolation between them.

Compared with a relay

No moving contacts: no arcing, no wear, silent, and fast. These were the relay's problems on Day 1.

Cautions

An SSR gets warm and may need a heat sink. When one fails, it usually fails on, so it is never the only safety stop.

Wrap Up

What We Know, and What Comes Next

The interface

An optocoupler isolates the logic, a transistor or MOSFET amplifies the signal, and a flyback diode protects the switch from the coil.

Power switches

A MOSFET follows its gate on DC. An SCR latches and turns off at zero current. A TRIAC switches AC in both directions.
