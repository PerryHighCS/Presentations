Inside an Induction Motor · Topic 01

Inside an Induction Motor

The stator is the stationary part: a core of thin, laminated iron sheets with copper windings laid in slots. The rotor turns on bearings inside it, separated by a narrow air gap.

There are no brushes and no wires to the rotor. The stator's changing field induces current in the rotor, which is why it is called an induction motor.
Inside an Induction Motor · Topic 01 · A Real Motor

A Real Induction Motor

This motor has been taken apart, with the rotor slid out of the stator. When it is assembled, the rotor sits inside the copper windings with only a narrow air gap between them.

  • The bearings keep the shaft centered, so the spinning rotor never rubs the stator.
  • The fan on the shaft blows air over the finned frame to carry away heat from the windings.
Inside an Induction Motor · Topic 01 · The Rotor

The Squirrel Cage Rotor

Take away the iron and the rotor's conductors look like a hamster wheel: bars shorted together by an end ring at each end.

The stator's changing field induces voltage in the bars, so current circulates around the cage. Current in a magnetic field feels a force, and that force turns the rotor.

The small DC motors from AR1 use permanent magnets and brushes. A cage rotor has neither, so it is rugged, cheap, and nearly maintenance free.
The Starting Problem · Topic 02

One Winding Makes a Pulsating Field

Connect single phase AC to the MAIN winding. The field (green arrow, B) grows, collapses, reverses, and repeats, but it always points up or down along one axis.

field shown 120× slower than 60 Hz
Think: the rotor is sitting still. Which way should it start turning: clockwise or counterclockwise?
The Starting Problem · Topic 02 · No Starting Torque

Why It Hums Instead of Turning

A pulsating field is the same as two half-size fields spinning in opposite directions: one clockwise, one counterclockwise. Add them and you get the green arrow.

  • At a standstill each half pulls the rotor equally hard in opposite directions, so the net starting torque is zero. The motor just hums.
  • Give the shaft a push either way and the half turning that way wins. The motor runs, in whichever direction you pushed.
  • Every single phase motor needs a starting mechanism that favors one direction.
The Starting Problem · Topic 02 · The Fix

Add a Second Winding, Shift Its Current

Put an AUX (auxiliary) winding 90° around the stator from MAIN. If its current peaks at a different time, the two fields combine into one that rotates. The faint trail shows the path of the arrow's tip.

The more the currents are shifted, up to 90°, the rounder the field and the stronger the starting torque. Each starting design is a different way to make that shift.

Five Starting Designs · Topic 03 · 1 of 5

The Shaded Pole Motor

A copper ring around one corner of each pole is a shorted turn. Current induced in the ring delays the field in that corner, so the field sweeps across the pole face toward the shaded part, and the rotor follows.

ProsExtremely simple and cheap: no capacitor, no switch, no extra winding.

ConsVery low starting torque and poor efficiency.

UsesSmall fans, bathroom exhaust fans, and other low power appliances.

Five Starting Designs · Topic 03 · 2 of 5

The Resistance Start Split Phase Motor

The AUX winding uses thinner wire, so it has more resistance than MAIN. Its current peaks earlier, roughly 25 to 30° ahead: a modest shift and a weak rotating field.

At about 75% speed a centrifugal switch on the shaft opens and drops the AUX winding out, because it would overheat if left on.

ProsInexpensive, with moderate starting torque.

UsesSome small pumps, tools, and machinery.

Five Starting Designs · Topic 03 · 3 of 5

The Capacitor Start Motor

A capacitor in series with AUX pushes its current to peak nearly 90° ahead of MAIN. That is close to the ideal shift, so the field rotates strongly and the starting torque is high.

The capacitor does not deliver an extra burst of electricity. Its job is to change the timing of the AUX current.

ConsThe start capacitor and switch add cost and parts that can fail.

UsesCompressors, pumps, and machines that start under heavy load.

Five Starting Designs · Topic 03 · 4 and 5 of 5

Keeping a Capacitor While Running

Permanent split capacitor (PSC)

A smaller run capacitor stays connected all the time. Quiet and efficient, but low to moderate starting torque. Used in fans and blowers.

Capacitor start capacitor run (CSCR)

A start capacitor for a strong start, then a run capacitor for efficient running. Best of both, at the highest cost.
