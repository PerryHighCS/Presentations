Brushed and Brushless · Topic 01 · Review

The Brushed DC Motor

You met the commutator in the generator lesson. In a motor it reverses the coil current every half turn, so the magnets keep pushing the coil the same direction.

ProsCheap and simple: apply DC and it spins. More voltage=more speed.

ConsBrushes rub and spark, so they wear out, waste energy, and make electrical noise.

UsesToys, small appliances, and the motors in your AR1 kits.

Brushed and Brushless · Topic 01 · Brushless Motors

The Brushless Motor Turns It Inside Out

A brushless DC (BLDC) motor swaps the parts around: the magnets spin on the rotor, and the windings stay still on the stator. Nothing needs to rub.

Electronic speed controller (ESC)

Transistors take over the commutator's job, switching current between the windings in the right order. DC goes in, timed pulses come out.

Rotor position sensing

To switch at the right moment, the ESC needs to know where the rotor is: Hall sensors detect the magnets, or it estimates position from the windings.

Gains

No brush wear, higher efficiency, and more power for its size.

Costs

Needs an ESC: more complex and more expensive to control.

Drones, cordless tools, computer fans, and electric cars all use brushless motors.
Brushed and Brushless · Topic 01 · Electronic Commutation

Electronic Commutation

The ESC energizes two phases at a time, making a stator field (green) a little ahead of the rotor magnet. The magnet turns toward it, the Hall sensors report the new position, and the ESC switches to the next pair.

Stepper Motors · Topic 02

A Stepper Moves in Steps

A stepper uses the same idea, but the driver does not wait for a sensor. Each pulse energizes the next phase, the field jumps, and the rotor snaps to line up. Count the pulses and you know the angle.

Rotor angle0°

This model has only 4 positions, so each full step is 90°. A real hybrid stepper has a toothed rotor with 50 teeth, which shrinks each step to 1.8°.

Stepper Motors · Topic 02 · Calculating Steps

Step Angle Calculations

Steps for a move steps = angle ÷ step angle

One revolution: 360° ÷ 1.8° = 200 steps

A 90° turn: 90° ÷ 1.8° = 50 steps

3D printer example

A belt moves the head 40 mm per revolution. One step moves it 40 ÷ 200 = 0.2 mm.

Microstepping

Drivers can split each step into smaller ones, such as 16 microsteps, for 3,200 positions per turn and smoother motion.

Stepper Motors · Topic 02 · Lost Steps

Open Loop Control and Lost Steps

Assumes it worked

The controller sent 50 steps, so it believes the shaft turned 90°.

Torque runs out

A stepper makes less torque at high speed. Too much load or too fast a start, and the rotor slips.

The error stays

Every later move is off by the same amount. On a 3D printer this is a layer shift.

Servo Systems · Topic 03

A Servo Is a Feedback System

A servo is not one kind of motor. It is a system: a sensor such as an encoder measures the actual position, the controller compares it with the command, and drives the motor to shrink the error.

The motor inside can be brushed, brushless, or even a stepper. What makes it a servo is the feedback that lets it detect an error.

Precision Under Pressure

A robot arm joint must swing to a target angle. You will give the same command to three systems, then add load and bump the arm to see which one still ends up on target.

A · Open loop stepper

Counts steps. No sensor. Cheapest and simplest.

B · Brushless, speed control

Holds a speed for a set time. Measures speed, not position.

C · Servo with encoder

Measures position and corrects error. Most complex.
