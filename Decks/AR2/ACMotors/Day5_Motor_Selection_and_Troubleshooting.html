Troubleshooting · Topic 01

A Repeatable Diagnostic Process

Real diagnosis starts with evidence, not guesses. Guessing replaces parts until something works, and it may never find the cause.

1Identify the symptom

What happens, and when? It hums, the breaker holds, and it ran last week.

2List possible causes

What could produce that symptom? Think through all the parts of the system.

3Choose a safe test

Which test tells the causes apart? Does it need lockout?

4Interpret the evidence

Compare each reading with the nameplate or a known good unit.

5Correct the cause

Fix the cause, not just the symptom. Never bypass protection.

6Verify safely

Remove the locks by procedure, restart, and measure again.

Troubleshooting · Topic 02

Troubleshooting Safely

Lockout / tagout

Before hands go near a machine, isolate every energy source, lock and tag it, and verify zero energy with a tested meter.

Stored energy

Capacitors and a VFD's DC bus stay charged after the power is off. Springs, gravity, and coasting loads can still move.

Qualified testing

Some readings need power on. Only trained, qualified people take them, with rated meters and protective gear.

An energized motor is never something to take apart, reach into, or adjust. In the work orders you choose the procedures, so read each one before you run it.
Troubleshooting · Topic 02

Lockout / Tagout (LOTO)

Lockout/tagout keeps a machine from starting, or releasing stored energy, while someone works on it. A switch that is only turned off can be turned back on.

  1. 1Notify. Tell everyone affected that the machine is going down.
  2. 2Shut down. Stop the machine with its normal controls.
  3. 3Isolate. Turn the disconnect switch to OFF, and shut off any air or hydraulic supply.
  4. 4Lock and tag. Each worker puts on their own lock and a tag with their name.
  5. 5Release stored energy. Discharge capacitors, wait out a drive's timer, and block parts that could fall.
  6. 6Verify zero energy. Press Start to prove nothing moves, then test for zero voltage with a meter.
Every worker adds their own lock, each with its own unique key, so the machine stays off until the last person is done. Only the person who put a lock on may take it off.
Troubleshooting · Fault Types

Three Kinds of Motor Faults

Mechanical

The machine fights the motor: worn bearings, binding, loose couplings, too much load, or blocked cooling.

Electrical

The power path fails: an open winding, a failed capacitor, a lost phase, or the wrong voltage.

Control

The instructions are wrong: drive settings, an overload set too low, too much acceleration, or a bad sensor.

The same symptom can come from any category. A pump that runs slow might have a binding impeller (mechanical), the wrong motor installed (electrical), or a wrong drive setting (control). Only evidence tells them apart.

Motor Maintenance Work Orders

Four machines are down: a ventilation fan, an industrial pump, a conveyor that keeps tripping, and an indexing slide that misses its target. Each one has a hidden cause.

1Investigate

Read the ticket and the machine history, then choose tests. Each test costs downtime: 2 min to read or observe, 5 to measure live, 15 to lock out.

2Diagnose

Strike out the causes your evidence rules out, then pick the most likely cause and a repair. Readings are evidence, not answers.

3Defend

Tag the readings that back your diagnosis. Your record scores six things, including safety. New fault hides a different cause.

Complete all four work orders. For each one, record on paper the tests you ran, the readings that mattered, your diagnosis and repair, and the safety step it required.

Unit Summary

What Determines What a Motor Can Do

Electrical supply

Single phase pulsates and needs a starting method. Three phase makes a rotating field by itself, and a VFD changes its frequency to change speed.

Motor construction

Windings, capacitors, poles, and the rotor set starting torque and speed. Steppers and brushless motors trade the squirrel cage for magnets and electronics.

Control and protection

Contactors, overloads, drives, and feedback decide how a motor starts, stops, holds a position, and is protected.

Every motor choice is a tradeoff, and every fault lives somewhere in this chain: the supply, the motor, the control, or the machine it drives.
