Why Can a Small Push Lift a Heavy Load?

In the syringe lab, a small cylinder made a large one push harder. Today we explain why, with numbers.

Predict

Make Your Predictions

A small water-filled cylinder is connected to a large one, and you push the small piston with 10 lb.

1. How hard does the large piston push?

2. How does the water pressure near the large piston compare with the pressure near the small one?

These are ungraded polls. Many students expect higher pressure at the large piston, because it pushes harder. Pascal’s Law says the pressure is the same everywhere; the extra force comes from the larger area.
Pressure

What Pressure Really Is

Pressure is how much force pushes on each square inch of a surface. Press your thumb on a table, and your push is spread over the small area of your thumb.

P=FA
pressure = force ÷ area

Force is in pounds (lb) and area is in square inches (in²), so pressure comes out in pounds per square inch: psi. That is the unit on almost every hydraulic and pneumatic gauge.

The same force on a smaller area makes a higher pressure. That is why a nail goes into wood point first.

Pascal’s Law

Pascal’s Law

Pascal’s Law: pressure applied to a confined fluid is passed equally to every part of that fluid.

Every connected piston, hose and cylinder feels the same pressure. A small piston and a large piston feel the same psi, but the large piston has more square inches for that pressure to push on, so it pushes harder.

The figure is the worked example on the next two slides. Point out that the pressure label is the same in both cylinders.
Worked Example

Step 1: Find the Pressure

You push a small piston with an area of 0.5 in² using a force of 10 lb. What pressure does the water feel?

P =FA
P =10 lb0.5 in²
P = 20 psi

By Pascal’s Law, that 20 psi now fills the entire system, every hose and every piston, no matter how large or small.

Before revealing the answer, ask students to predict whether the pressure will be more or less than 10. Dividing by an area smaller than 1 makes the number bigger.
Worked Example

Step 2: Find the Output Force

That 20 psi reaches a second piston with an area of 4 in². How hard does it push?

P =FA, so F = P × A
F = 20 psi × 4 in²
F = 80 lb

You supplied 10 lb, and the system pushes with 80 lb, because the output piston has 8 times the area. Each of its square inches gets the same 20 pounds of push.

The Trade-Off

Nothing for Free

The water that leaves the small cylinder has to fill the large one. The large cylinder is 8 times wider, so it rises only 1/8 as far.

Volume In = Volume Out

0.5 in² × 8 in = 4 in³

4 in² × 1 in = 4 in³

Push the small piston 8 in, and the large piston rises 1 in.

Work In = Work Out

10 lb × 8 in = 80 lb·in

80 lb × 1 in = 80 lb·in

Force times distance is the same on both sides, in an ideal system.

Hydraulics trades distance for force, just like a gear train or a pulley system. It never creates energy from nothing.

This explains the Station 3 observation: the large syringe moved less but pushed harder. Real systems lose a little to friction and leaks, so the work out is slightly less than the work in.

Design Problem

Designing a Lift

You want the output piston to lift 150 lb, and the output piston has an area of 3 in². Your input piston has an area of 0.5 in².

What pressure does the system need?
P =FA=1503
P = 50 psi
What input force makes 50 psi?
F = P × A = 50 × 0.5
F = 25 lb

Engineers design around this trade-off: a small input force lifts a large load, as long as you are willing to push farther.

Have partners solve each step before revealing it. This matches simulator challenge 2, so groups can check their simulator settings against it.
Your Turn

Try One

A car lift’s output piston has an area of 6 in² and must lift 240 lb. Its input piston has an area of 0.75 in².

1. What pressure does the fluid need?

2. What input force is needed to make that pressure?

Answers: 240 ÷ 6 = 40 psi, then 40 × 0.75 = 30 lb. The distractors come from multiplying when you should divide (1,440 psi), adding (246 psi), dividing upside down (0.025 psi), and using the wrong area (53 lb, 180 lb, 320 lb).

Extension

Extension: Area from Diameter

Real cylinders are labeled by their bore, the inside diameter, not their area. To use P = F ÷ A, first find the area of the circle. The radius is half the bore.

A = π × r²
r = radius = bore ÷ 2
A 2 in bore has a radius of 1 in:
A = 3.14 × 1²=3.14 in²
A 4 in bore has a radius of 2 in:
A = 3.14 × 2²=12.56 in²

Doubling the bore from 2 in to 4 in makes the area 4 times larger (3.14 to 12.56 in²), because the radius doubles and then is squared: 2² = 4.

Optional, for students ready for circle geometry. It comes back on Day 4 when students read cylinder bores. The rectangular piston problem on the practice sheet is a good warm-up: area = length × width.
VEX Pneumatics

The VEX Pneumatic System

The air tank stores compressed air at up to 100 psi.

The solenoid valve sends that air to one end of the cylinder or the other.

The cylinder turns air pressure into a push or a pull. Its bore is only 10 mm, about the width of a pencil.

This is the kit students will use in the VEXploration days. Pass a cylinder around if you can, so students see how small the bore is. By Pascal’s Law, the air pressure from the tank is the same everywhere in the connected tubing and cylinder, so the cylinder’s force depends only on that pressure and its small area. The next slide works out that force.
Worked Example

Worked Example: A VEX Pneumatic Cylinder

The VEX V5 cylinder from the last slide has a 10 mm bore, which is 0.394 in. How hard does it push when the air tank is at its 100 psi maximum, and what pressure does it need to push a 5 lb load?

Step 1: Find the area.
The radius is 0.394 ÷ 2 = 0.197 in.
A = π × r² = 3.14 × 0.197²
A ≈ 0.12 in²
Step 2: Find the force at 100 psi.
F = P × A = 100 × 0.12
F = 12 lb
Step 3: Find the pressure for a 5 lb load.
P =FA=5 lb0.12 in²
P ≈ 42 psi

The tank loses pressure each time a cylinder fires. As long as it stays above about 42 psi, this cylinder can still push the load.

VEX lists the V5 cylinder at a 10 mm bore and a 12 lb maximum force at 100 psi, which matches Step 2. Ask students to estimate first: the area is about an eighth of a square inch, so 100 psi gives about 12 lb. Point out the units: psi × in² = lb, and lb ÷ in² = psi. The 12 lb is a push, not a lifting rating: friction, the angle of the linkage, and a falling tank pressure all reduce what the cylinder can do on a robot. This sets up the VEXploration days, when students will see the cylinder weaken as the tank empties.
Exit Ticket

Before You Go

In a hydraulic jack, the output piston’s area is doubled. The push and the input piston stay the same.

1. What happens to the output force and to how far the output rises for the same push?

2. Explain your answer using pressure and the volume of fluid that moves.

The pressure is unchanged, so doubling the area doubles the force. The same volume spread over twice the area rises half as far. Work in still equals work out.
