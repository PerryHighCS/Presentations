Fluid Power in the Assembly Line

Project

How Engineers Automate a Line

Photo: De Man Automation + Service, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

Automation engineers rarely build a machine from one kind of part. They combine off-the-shelf parts, fluid power and custom parts made for one job.

Your station will do the same: a VEX structure, a pneumatic cylinder, and one part you design in Onshape and 3D print.

Plan Day 10. The robot from Deck 1 is a reminder that factory stations mix structure, air and custom tooling. Standards: 7.2 and 7.9, with 16.x design process connections.
Your Mission

The Challenge

Your team will design, build and document a small mechanism that works as a single station on an automated assembly line.

The core challenge: your station must use pneumatic power to do a physical task, and it must include a custom 3D printed part that helps it do that task.

A station does one job well: it pushes, clamps, lifts, sorts or holds a product so the next station can work on it.

Read the mission together. Teams are formed before this lesson. Over the next lessons teams plan, build, test, write an operator manual and demonstrate.
Constraints

What Your Station Must Do

Mechanism

Fits on one VEX base plate and uses a linkage or movement system: a lever, hinge, rail, turntable or clamp frame.

Pneumatics

At least one cylinder and one solenoid valve, with speed-controlled motion from a flow fitting.

Printed part

One custom part that does work and fits inside a 4 in × 4 in × 4 in box.

The operator must be kept safe from pinch points. The station does not need to run itself: a manual trigger is fine.

From the project brief, sections A to C. Speed control uses the flow fittings from Deck 8; the linkage can be the pivoting arm from Deck 9 or a rail, clamp or turntable. Breaking a constraint costs points.

Inspiration

Station Ideas

Stuck? Start from an idea like these, then make it your own.

The Box Pusher

A cylinder pushes products off a conveyor. Printed part: a pusher foot sized to the product.

Clamping Station

A cylinder holds a part still for drilling or labeling. Printed part: a V-block or curved jaw.

Ejector Gate

A cylinder raises or lowers a gate to stop the flow. Printed part: the gate frame or a hinge bracket.

Stamp Press

A cylinder presses a design onto a product. Printed part: the press head with a shape.

From the brief’s inspiration table. Point out the movement system in each: a rail or straight push (pusher, press), a hinge (gate), a clamp frame (clamp).
Inspiration

More Station Ideas

Alignment Slider

A cylinder pushes a product along a rail. Printed part: a carriage that fits the rail.

The Gripper

A cylinder closes a claw to grab an object. Printed part: fingertips with grooves or teeth.

Diverter Arm

A cylinder swings an arm to sort items left or right. Printed part: a curved paddle.

Safety Lock

A cylinder slides a pin in to lock a gear. Printed part: a guide channel for the pin.

The pivoting arm from the last lesson already works as a diverter or a gate.

Slider: a rail; gripper: a linkage that turns a push into a squeeze; diverter: a pivoting arm (Deck 9); lock: a straight push into a channel.

Feedback

Pitch It to Another Team

Pair up with another team. Each team gets two minutes to pitch its chosen concept. The listening team asks these questions, and the pitching team records the answers in its notebook.

Where is the pinch point, and how will you keep the operator’s hands out of it?
What does your printed part do that a VEX part cannot?
Which stroke will you slow down, and where does the flow fitting go?
Plan Day 10’s peer critique. The third question checks Deck 8: the fitting goes on the port that empties during the stroke you want slow.
