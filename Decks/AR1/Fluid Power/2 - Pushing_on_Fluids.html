Safety

Safety Rules for Fluid Power

These rules apply to every fluid power system in this unit, from today’s syringes to the VEX pneumatics kit.

1Wear safety glasses whenever a system is filled or pressurized.
2Never point a cylinder, syringe or open line at anyone, including yourself.
3Check that every connection is secure before you push or pressurize.
4Release the pressure before you change, disconnect or adjust anything.
5Keep fingers clear of pinch points where a moving part meets a fixed one.
6Wipe up spills right away, because wet floors and tables are slippery.
ADE 7.4. These come from the original Day 1 safety slides, rewritten as rules students can check themselves against. Manufacturer pressure limits come up again with the VEX kit, which is rated to 100 psi.
Safety

Two Different Dangers

Pneumatic: Stored Energy

Compressed air stores energy like a squeezed spring. If a line or fitting fails, that energy releases all at once, so a part can burst or a loose hose can whip around.

Hydraulic: Pinhole Leaks

Oil stores little energy, but real hydraulic systems run at very high pressure. A pinhole leak sprays a jet thin enough to inject oil through skin, and the injury can look minor while it badly damages the hand.

Never feel for a hydraulic leak with your hand. Check with a piece of cardboard or a paper towel instead. Today’s syringes are low pressure, but this is the habit to build now.
This corrects the old Day 3 slide that described leaking as the only hydraulic risk. A fluid injection injury is a medical emergency even when the wound looks small.

Lab Stations

Three Stations

Your team will rotate through three stations. At each one, follow the posted directions, measure carefully, and record what you observe on your data sheet, even when it surprises you.

Station 1

Air vs. Water

Push on two connected syringes filled with air, then with water, and compare how each one responds.

Station 2

Equal Syringes

Push one of two same-size, water-filled syringes and watch how the other one shares the push.

Station 3

Small vs. Large

Push a small syringe connected to a large one, then reverse it, and compare distance and force.

Stations use the PHS Day 2 lab directions and data sheet. Have paper towels and a cup of water at every station, and keep the syringe sizes at Station 2 matched.

Station 1

Air vs. Water: Which One Is Squishy?

What to do

Mark centimeters on both barrels. Connect two same-size syringes filled with air, have a partner hold the output barrel, and push the input 1 cm at a time.

Then fill the input syringe and tubing with water, push out as many bubbles as you can, and repeat.

What to watch for
  • Does the output move right away, or after a delay?
  • Does the push feel firm, or soft and springy?
  • What happens when you let go of the input?
The air system should show a delay, a springy feel, and a bounce back when released. The water system should respond almost immediately. Remind students that leftover bubbles make water act a little like air.
Station 2

Equal Syringes: Sharing the Push

What to do

With two same-size syringes full of water, one partner pushes the input 1 cm while the other watches the output. Keep pushing 1 cm at a time, then switch roles.

Finally, set both plungers near the middle and gently push toward each other at the same time.

What to watch for
  • Does the output always move the same distance as the input?
  • Does the push feel the same to both partners?
  • What happens when both of you push at once?
Equal syringes should move equal distances and feel the same, almost like a solid rod. When both partners push, neither plunger moves much: the water passes each push to the other side.
Station 3

Small vs. Large: A Mini Hydraulic Jack

What to do

Fill a small syringe with water and connect it to a large syringe that starts pushed all the way in. Push the small plunger 1 cm, then switch and push the large plunger 1 cm.

If you have time, set a small load on the large plunger and try to lift it by pushing the small one.

What to watch for
  • How far does the other plunger move each time?
  • Which plunger is harder to hold still?
  • What do you give up to get a bigger push?
Pushing the small syringe 1 cm moves the large one less than 1 cm, but the large one pushes back much harder. Pushing the large one moves the small one farther. This is the force and distance trade-off from gears and pulleys, which Day 3 explains with Pascal’s Law.

Patterns

What the Syringes Showed

Air Squeezes

Part of your push went into squeezing the air, so the output moved late and less. The squeezed air stored energy and pushed back when you let go.

Water Passes the Push

Water barely squeezes, so the volume you pushed out of one syringe went into the other almost instantly. Equal syringes moved equal distances.

With a small and a large syringe, the large one moved less but pushed harder. You traded distance for force, just like a gear train or a pulley system. Tomorrow’s question is why.
Water is approximately incompressible here, not perfectly. The trade-off links back to mechanical advantage from the Mechanical Linkages unit. Day 3 explains it with Pascal’s Law and P = F / A.
Hydraulic Circuit

You Built a Hydraulic Circuit

Each syringe is a simple cylinder, and its plunger is the piston that slides inside it.

The input cylinder does the job of a pump, the tubing is a hydraulic line, and the output cylinder is an actuator that moves a load.

In your notebook, draw and label your Station 3 circuit. Add your measurements and one sentence about how well it worked as a hydraulic jack.
ADE 7.8 asks students to build and examine a hydraulic circuit. This notebook entry is the evidence: an assembled circuit, a labeled diagram, measurements, and a performance conclusion.
Troubleshooting

The Station 2 Service Call

Station 2 is acting up: pushing the input 2 cm moves the output only about 1.4 cm. Something is wrong, and your job is to find out what without guessing.

Step 1

Investigate

Read the ticket, the station log, and what each cause would look like. Then choose tests, which cost class time.

Step 2

Diagnose

Strike out the causes your evidence rules out, then pick the cause and a fix.

Step 3

Defend

Tag the readings that back your diagnosis. Your record scores six things, including safety.

Exit Ticket

Before You Go

At Station 3, a small cylinder pushed water into a large one. Why can the larger piston push with more force but travel less distance?

Look for answers that mention the volume of water moving from one syringe to the other, or a trade-off between force and distance. Day 3 builds the full explanation with Pascal’s Law.
