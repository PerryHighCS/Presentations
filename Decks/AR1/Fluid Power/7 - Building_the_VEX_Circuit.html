Power Supply

Where the Air Comes From

A VEX robot carries no compressor. The classroom compressor fills the tank through its fill valve before a run, and the robot runs on that stored air.

TankStores the compressed air, up to 100 psi.
RegulatorSends out a steady, lower pressure; its gauge shows how much.
Shut-off valveOpens or closes the supply by hand, and vents what is after it.
Solenoid valveSends air to A or B when the Brain switches its signal.
CylinderTurns the air’s pressure into a push or a pull.

Every push uses up some of the stored air, so the tank pressure drops during a run. The regulator keeps the cylinder’s pressure steady until the tank falls below its setting.

ADE 7.7, power supplies: compare with Deck 4’s industrial chain (compressor, receiver, regulator) and the hydraulic pump and reservoir. The VEX chain is the same idea with the compressor left behind. Ask: what happens to the force near the end of a match? (The regulated pressure holds until the tank drops below 50 psi, then force falls.) Callback to Deck 4’s Boyle’s Law: a full 100 psi tank holds only about 7.8 tanks’ worth of room air, and each stroke spends some of it.
Safety

Rules for Building

1

Wear safety glasses whenever the tank holds air, yours or a neighbor’s.

2

Build with the tank empty and the shut-off valve closed. Air goes in only when your teacher checks the circuit.

3

Tighten fittings by hand only. Tools crack the threads and damage the O-ring seals.

4

Never fill the tank above 100 psi. Today we test with the regulator at 50 psi.

5

Point the rod away from people, and keep fingers out of its path. A cylinder can move the moment air reaches it.

ADE 7.4. Rule 3 comes from VEX’s own documentation. Rule 5 matters at the first pressurization: the VEX solenoid always sends air to A or B, so the cylinder moves as soon as the shut-off valve opens, even before anyone touches the program.
Safety

Making the Circuit Safe to Touch

Before you touch a tube or fitting on a charged circuit, close the shut-off valve. Closing it blocks the supply and vents the air after it, so the solenoid valve, the cylinder and their tubes are safe.

Switching the signal is not enough. The VEX solenoid always sends air to A or B, so the cylinder stays pressurized in its last position. Stopping the program does not vent it either.

The tank and regulator, before the shut-off valve, stay charged. Empty the tank with your teacher before you take the tank side apart or put the kit away.

ADE 7.4, depressurizing. The plan’s safety note: never assume switching off a solenoid has exhausted the system. Show your classroom procedure for emptying the tank (the plan records that VEX documentation does not give one).
Safety Check

Before You Build

1. Your team needs to move a tube on a circuit that is already pressurized. What do you do first?

  1. APull the tube out quickly so the air escapes all at once
  2. BSwitch the signal to low so the valve exhausts the air
  3. CLoosen the fitting with pliers
  4. DClose the shut-off valve, which vents the air after it

2. A VEX fitting is leaking a little. How should you tighten it?

  1. ABy hand only; tools can crack the threads and damage the seals
  2. BWith pliers, as tight as it will go
  3. CWith a wrench, then back it off a quarter turn
  4. DRaise the pressure so the fitting seats itself
Q1: close the shut-off valve (switching the signal leaves the cylinder pressurized; pulling a charged tube can whip). Q2: by hand only. Every student should get both before teams get parts.
Build

Connecting Tubes

VEX fittings are push-to-connect: a tube pushed in far enough is gripped and sealed, with no tools.

1

Cut the tube straight across, long enough to reach without kinking or pulling.

2

Push it into the fitting until it stops, then tug gently. It should not come out.

3

To remove a tube, close the shut-off valve first. Then press the collar in and pull the tube out.

4

A steady hiss at a fitting means a leak. Close the shut-off valve, then push the tube in farther.

Demonstrate on one fitting before teams start. An angled cut or a tube that stops short of the seal is the usual cause of a leak. The colored ring on each fitting is the release collar.
Build

Build the Circuit

Follow your schematic from last lesson, in the order the air travels.

1

Tank outlet to the regulator.

2

Regulator to the shut-off valve, its arrow pointing toward the solenoid, dial closed.

3

Shut-off valve to solenoid port P. Leave the spare P port plugged.

4

Port A to the rod end and port B to the cap end.

5

Driver cable: red and black on the A side, plugged into the Brain’s 3-wire port.

6

Stop. Your teacher checks the circuit before any air goes in.

ADE 7.9. The A-to-rod-end routing matches Deck 5 and the classroom program, so a low signal holds the cylinder retracted. Tell teams which 3-wire port the classroom program uses. Check every circuit for: shut-off arrow direction, the spare P plug, tubes fully seated, plug colors, and the rod pointed away from people.
Predict

Before the Air Goes In

Your team ran the A tube to the rod end and the B tube to the cap end. The classroom program starts with a low signal. When you open the shut-off valve, what will the cylinder do?

  1. AExtend, because the supply air pushes the piston out
  2. BRetract, or stay retracted, because low sends air to A and the rod end
  3. CNothing until the program switches the signal
  4. DMove halfway and stop
Answer: it retracts, or stays retracted. The VEX solenoid always sends air to A or B; with a low signal, air goes to A and the rod end. “Nothing until the program switches” is the common wrong idea, and it is also why hands must be clear when the shut-off valve opens.
Test

Pressurize and Test

1

Glasses on. Your teacher fills the tank, no higher than 100 psi.

2

Set the regulator to 50 psi on its gauge.

3

Hands clear, rod pointed away. Open the shut-off valve and watch the cylinder.

4

Listen. A steady hiss is a leak: close the shut-off valve and fix it.

5

Run the classroom program and switch the signal between low and high.

6

Record each test in your notebook. Close the shut-off valve before any change.

Step 3 is the prediction check: the cylinder should retract or stay retracted as soon as the shut-off valve opens. A short hiss from R when the signal switches is normal (the exhaust); a steady hiss with nothing moving is a leak.

Troubleshooting

The Cylinder That Won’t Move

Team 4 finished their build, but when they switch the signal from low to high, the cylinder does not move at all. Find out why without guessing, and without breaking a safety rule.

Step 1

Investigate

Read the ticket, the build log, and what each cause would look like. Tests cost build time.

Step 2

Diagnose

Strike out the causes your evidence rules out, then pick the cause and a fix.

Step 3

Defend

Tag the readings that back your diagnosis. Your record scores six things, including safety.

Safety

Shutting Down

1

Close the shut-off valve. The solenoid valve, cylinder and their tubes vent.

2

Stop the program. The cylinder no longer holds any pressure, so it can be moved by hand.

3

The tank is still charged. Empty it with your teacher before you store the kit or open the tank side.

4

Leave the circuit built for the next lesson, unless your teacher says to take it apart.

ADE 7.4. Step 2 is safe only because step 1 vented the downstream side first. Decide whether teams keep their circuits built: the next lesson adds flow controls to the same circuit.
